Finding better clues for a tricky kind of breast cancer 🔎

The same project as the technical flow page, but explained the simple way — no jargon, just the idea.
Doctors check three little "tags" on a breast cancer tumor to help pick medicine. Triple-negative means all three tags say "no" — so the usual medicines don't have a target to aim at. That makes it harder to treat, and it's why scientists want to find new, better clues hidden inside the tumor's genes to help sort it into smaller groups that each respond to different treatments.
📚

1. Collect clues from real cases Done

Scientists around the world already studied hundreds of real patients (kept anonymous) and shared the results for free, like a big public library of clues — things like the tumor's size, and which genes were turned "up" or "down" inside it.

We gathered 320 of these TNBC cases to start with.

↓
🕵️

2. Teach a computer detective to spot patterns Done

We built a computer program that looks at all those clues and guesses which family group a tumor belongs to — kind of like sorting Lego bricks by color, but the "color" is hidden in genes instead of something you can see.

Our detective guesses right about 92 out of 100 times — much better than guessing from tumor size alone (about 61 out of 100).

↓
🙋

3. Make the detective show its work Done

We don't just trust a guess — we ask "why did you think that?" The computer points to the exact genes it used to decide, and happily, those are genes doctors already know matter for this cancer. That's like checking your math homework and seeing the steps make sense.

↓
🔬

4. Give the detective eyes, too Done

We fetched real microscope photos of tumor tissue and showed them to a ready-made "vision expert" AI that already learned to read medical photos, so it could describe each photo as a list of numbers (like a fingerprint for what's in the picture).

Then we asked an honest question: do photos that look similar to each other also turn out to have similar gene clues? For now, with the small set of photos we have, the answer is not really — no clear link yet. We're saying that plainly instead of pretending it worked, because that's how real science stays trustworthy.

↓
🎮

5. Build a toy anyone can try Done

We built a live web app: pick a patient, and watch the detective make its guess, show a confidence percentage, and explain exactly which clues convinced it — instead of only reading about it. You can even drag sliders to change a clue and watch the guess update instantly.

↓
📣
